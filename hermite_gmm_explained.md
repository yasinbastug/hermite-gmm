# The Hermite Gaussian Mixture Model, Explained From Scratch

*A ground-up walkthrough of the proposal — every mathematical concept, why it's there, and what it buys you.*

---

## How to read this

This document explains a single idea: **take a Gaussian mixture model, and let each cluster wear a learnable "lens" that reshapes it.**

Everything else — Radon–Nikodym derivatives, Hermite polynomials, whitening transforms, orthonormal bases — is machinery in service of that one idea. My goal is that by the end you understand not just *what* each piece does but *why it has to be there*, i.e. what would break if you removed it.

Each section follows the same pattern: the problem that forces the concept, the concept explained intuitively, the formal statement, and then the specific job it does in this model.

# Part I — The Problem

## 1. What a mixture model is trying to do

You have data points $x_1,\dots,x_n$ in $\mathbb{R}^k$. You believe they came from several distinct sub-populations — clusters — but you weren't told which point came from which. You want to recover both the clusters' shapes and the assignment of points to clusters.

The mixture-model answer: assume each data point was generated by a two-stage process.

1. **Pick a cluster.** Roll a weighted die with $C$ faces. Face $c$ comes up with probability $\pi_c$.
2. **Draw a point from that cluster's distribution.** If you got face $c$, sample $x \sim p_c$.

If you marginalize out the die roll (i.e. average over which face came up), the density of the observed $x$ is

$$p(x) = \sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_c\, p_c(x)$$

This is a **mixture density**. The $\pi_c$ are the **mixing weights**, and they must satisfy $\pi_c \ge 0$ and $\sum_c \pi_c = 1$ — they're probabilities of a categorical variable.

The die roll is a **latent variable**: real, but unobserved. Call it $z_i \in \{1,\dots,C\}$ for point $i$. Almost everything in the fitting algorithm later is about reasoning backwards from the observed $x_i$ to a belief about the hidden $z_i$.

## 2. The Gaussian choice, and where it fails

The standard choice is $p_c = \mathcal{N}(\mu_c, \Sigma_c)$:

$$q_c(x) = \mathcal{N}(x;\mu_c,\Sigma_c) = (2\pi)^{-k/2}|\Sigma_c|^{-1/2}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12 (x-\mu_c)^\top\Sigma_c^{-1}(x-\mu_c)\right)$$

Two parameters per cluster, with clean geometric meanings:

- $\mu_c \in \mathbb{R}^k$ — **where** the cluster sits.
- $\Sigma_c$ — a $k\times k$ symmetric positive-definite matrix controlling **how big and which way** the cluster is stretched.

### Unpacking $\Sigma_c$

Since this matrix carries all the shape information, it's worth slowing down on what it actually holds.

**What's stored in it.** $\Sigma_c$ is a table of pairwise relationships between coordinates:

- **Diagonal entry $\Sigma_{jj}$** — the variance of coordinate $j$. Its square root is the standard deviation, i.e. the typical spread along axis $j$.
- **Off-diagonal entry $\Sigma_{jl}$** — the covariance between coordinates $j$ and $l$. Positive means they tend to be large together; negative means one is large when the other is small; zero means no linear relationship. This is what **tilts** the cloud.

It's symmetric because the relationship between $j$ and $l$ is the same as between $l$ and $j$. "Positive definite" is the requirement that the cloud has genuine spread in every direction — no direction in which it's perfectly flat.

**The easy case.** If all off-diagonals are zero, the cloud is axis-aligned. In 2-D, $\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}9 & 0\\ 0 & 4\end{pmatrix}$ gives an ellipse centered at $\mu$, extending $\sqrt9 = 3$ units along $x$ and $\sqrt4 = 2$ units along $y$. Read the shape straight off the diagonal.

**Why off-diagonals complicate this.** Once they're nonzero, the diagonal no longer tells you the shape. Consider

$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 3\\ 3 & 5\end{pmatrix}$$

The diagonal says the spread is identical along $x$ and $y$ (both variance 5, sd 2.24), which sounds like a circle. It is not a circle. The off-diagonal 3 means the coordinates are correlated ($\text{corr} = 3/5 = 0.6$), so the cloud is a long ellipse tilted at 45°.

**Now the eigen-language.** The variance in any direction $d$ (a unit vector) is $d^\top\Sigma d$. Sweep $d$ around the circle and you get different answers:

| direction $d$ | variance $d^\top\Sigma d$ | sd |
|---|---|---|
| along $x$ | 5.0 | 2.24 |
| along $y$ | 5.0 | 2.24 |
| along $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ — the 45° diagonal | **8.0** | **2.83** |
| along $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$ — the anti-diagonal | **2.0** | **1.41** |

The maximum and minimum occur along the 45° and 135° directions. Those two special directions are the **eigenvectors**, and the variances there — 8 and 2 — are the **eigenvalues**. They are exactly the ellipse's own axes: the directions in which the cloud is purely stretched, with no tilt left over.

So the original sentence unpacks to:

- **"Eigenvectors are the principal axes"** = the eigenvectors point along the long and short directions of the elliptical cloud. Here: 45° (long) and 135° (short).
- **"Square roots of eigenvalues are the axis lengths"** = eigenvalues are *variances* along those axes, and lengths are measured in standard deviations, so you take square roots. Here: $\sqrt8 = 2.83$ and $\sqrt2 = 1.41$, a roughly 2:1 elongated ellipse.

**The takeaway.** Every covariance matrix says the same kind of thing: *rotate to these axes, then stretch by these amounts.* Rotation plus scaling — that's the complete vocabulary. Section 12 uses precisely this decomposition in reverse, to *undo* the rotation and stretching and turn any cluster back into a standard sphere.

The level sets of $q_c$ — the contours of constant density — are ellipsoids. That's the entire expressive vocabulary of a Gaussian component: **it can be any ellipsoid, and nothing else.**

Real clusters are frequently not ellipsoids. They can be:

- **Skewed** — a long tail on one side, a sharp edge on the other. Reaction times, incomes, waiting times.
- **Heavy-tailed** — occasional far-flung points that a Gaussian assigns essentially zero probability.
- **Mildly curved** — banana-shaped, arising naturally from nonlinear relationships between variables.
- **Peaked or flat** relative to a Gaussian (excess kurtosis).

### Why "just add more components" is a bad answer

You *can* approximate a banana with five small Gaussians. GMMs are universal approximators in that sense. But this destroys the thing you wanted the model for:

- **Interpretability collapses.** You asked for clusters; you got five fragments of one cluster. The correspondence "one component = one meaningful group" is gone, and there's no automatic way to know which fragments belong together.
- **Model selection breaks.** BIC or held-out likelihood will now select a component count that reflects *shape complexity*, not *how many groups there are*. Those are different questions and you've conflated them.
- **Parameters are spent badly.** Five 2-D Gaussians cost $5\times(2+3)+4 = 29$ parameters to express one skewed blob. A skew parameter or two would have done it.

  *Where the 29 comes from:* each Gaussian component in $k=2$ dimensions needs a mean $\mu_c\in\mathbb{R}^2$ (2 numbers) and a covariance $\Sigma_c$, which is a symmetric $2\times2$ matrix — 2 diagonal entries plus 1 off-diagonal, so 3 independent numbers (this is the $k(k+1)/2$ count from Section 12's discussion of $\Sigma_c$). That's $2+3=5$ numbers per component. Five components: $5\times5=25$. Then add the mixing weights $\pi_1,\dots,\pi_5$ — only 4 of them are free, since they must sum to 1 and the last is determined by the rest. Total: $25+4=29$. This is exactly the parameter-counting formula used later for BIC in Section 36 ($d_{\text{eff}} = (C{-}1)+Ck+C\tfrac{k(k+1)}{2}$), just worked out for $C=5,k=2$ ahead of time. The contrast: a single Hermite-GMM component with one extra skew coefficient reaches for similar shape flexibility at $5 + 1 = 6$ parameters total, instead of 29 spread across five redundant components.

So the goal is sharp: **keep one component per real cluster, but make a component able to be a non-ellipsoid.**

## 3. The design constraints

Any fix has to respect four things, or it isn't worth having:

1. **It must remain a valid probability density.** Non-negative everywhere, integrating to exactly 1. This is not a formality — the whole apparatus of maximum likelihood, responsibilities, and model comparison assumes it.
2. **The normalizing constant must be computable in closed form.** If normalizing requires a numerical integral over $\mathbb{R}^k$ that changes every time a parameter moves, the model is unusable. This constraint quietly kills most naive approaches and is the single strongest force shaping the final design.

   *What this means concretely, and where it resurfaces:* "normalizing constant" here refers to whatever factor makes $\int \rho_c(u)\,d\gamma_k(u) = 1$ hold — the requirement from Section 5 below. For an arbitrary positive function $h(u)$, the true normalizer is $Z = \int h(u)\,d\gamma_k(u)$, and you'd use $h(u)/Z$ as your density. The trouble is computing $Z$: it's a $k$-dimensional integral, it has no closed form for most choices of $h$, and — critically — it changes every time you update the coefficients defining $h$ during optimization. Recomputing it by numerical integration at every gradient step, for every one of the $C$ components, at every EM iteration, is what "unusable" means in practice.

   This single requirement is the thread that ties together most of the document's technical choices:
   - **Section 21** shows the natural elegant choice — an exponential correction $\rho_c \propto \exp(\sum_\alpha b_\alpha\phi_\alpha)$ — dies on exactly this point: its normalizer $Z(b)$ has no closed form and can even diverge.
   - **Section 22** is the resolution: squaring the correction and using an orthonormal basis makes the normalizer collapse, via Parseval's identity (Section 16), into $B_c=\sum_\alpha b_{c,\alpha}^2$ — a finite sum of squares, computable in closed form for *any* coefficient vector, no integral ever performed. That's constraint 2, satisfied.
   - **Section 12's whitening** and **Section 17's Hermite basis** are both prerequisites for that trick to work at all — they're what make the orthonormality (and hence Parseval) available in the first place.
   - **Section 34's algorithm** is only tractable per-iteration because the normalizer is a cheap sum rather than a quadrature call; this is what keeps the per-iteration cost in Section 34 at $O(nC|\mathcal{A}_m|)$ rather than something involving numerical integration.
   - **Part X (Section 37)** generalizes the same requirement to other reference measures: whatever basis you pick, it must be orthonormal so the same closed-form collapse happens.

   In short: nearly every "why not just do the obvious thing" moment later in the document — Section 20's failed additive attempt, Section 21's failed exponential attempt — is this constraint ruling out an option, and Section 22 is this constraint finally being satisfied.
3. **It must strictly contain the GMM.** There should be a specific parameter setting that recovers the ordinary Gaussian mixture exactly. This gives a safe initialization, a nested-model comparison, and a guarantee you can't do worse.
4. **It must fit the EM computational pattern.** Iterate: soft-assign points to clusters, then update each cluster from its assigned points. This is what makes mixture models tractable at all.

Hold onto constraint 2 in particular. Almost every strange-looking choice later — the whitening, the orthonormal basis, the squaring — exists to satisfy it.

# Part II — The Central Idea

## 4. A lens on top of a Gaussian

Here is the whole proposal in one line:

$$p_c(x) = \underbrace{q_c(x)}_{\text{Gaussian}} \times \underbrace{\rho_c(u_c(x))}_{\text{correction}}$$

Don't replace the Gaussian — **multiply** it by a learned positive function $\rho_c$ that reshapes it.

The intuition is genuinely optical. The Gaussian $q_c$ is a spotlight casting a smooth elliptical pool of light. The correction $\rho_c$ is a piece of patterned glass held in front of it. Where $\rho_c > 1$ the glass brightens that region — the model puts *more* mass there than a Gaussian would. Where $\rho_c < 1$ it dims — *less* mass. Where $\rho_c = 1$ the Gaussian shows through untouched.

The division of labor is deliberate:

| Component | Handles | Parameters |
|---|---|---|
| $q_c$ — the Gaussian | Coarse geometry: location, scale, orientation | $\mu_c$, $\Sigma_c$ |
| $\rho_c$ — the correction | Fine shape: skew, tails, asymmetry, local detail | $b_c$ (Hermite coefficients) |

And $u_c(x) = \Sigma_c^{-1/2}(x-\mu_c)$ is the **local coordinate system** — we'll unpack it in Part IV. The correction is expressed in *cluster-relative* coordinates, not raw data coordinates, so each cluster gets its own private frame of reference.

The key structural property: setting $\rho_c \equiv 1$ gives back exactly $p_c = q_c$, an ordinary Gaussian component. Constraint 3 is satisfied by construction.

## 5. What the correction must satisfy

For $p_c = q_c \cdot \rho_c$ to be a density, we need $\int p_c(x)\,dx = 1$. Since $q_c(x)\,dx$ is already a probability measure, this says:

$$\rho_c \ge 0 \quad\text{everywhere}, \qquad \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q_c}[\rho_c(u_c(x))] = 1$$

**"$\rho_c$ must be non-negative and average to exactly 1 under the Gaussian."**

That "exactly" is the hard part. It has to hold for *every* value of the coefficient vector $b_c$ the optimizer might try, automatically, without anyone computing an integral. The remainder of the mathematical machinery is the answer to how.

# Part III — Measure Theory: The Language for "Density Relative To"

This section explains where the correction comes from conceptually. You can build the model without it, but you'd have no idea why it should work, and no idea how to generalize it (Part X depends entirely on this).

## 6. Measures: assigning size to sets

A **measure** is a rule $\mu$ that assigns a non-negative size $\mu(A)$ to sets $A$, additively: disjoint sets' sizes add. Familiar examples:

- **Lebesgue measure** on $\mathbb{R}^k$ — ordinary geometric volume. Length in 1D, area in 2D, volume in 3D. Written $dx$. Total mass is infinite.
- **A probability measure** — total mass exactly 1. $P(A)$ = "probability the random point lands in $A$."
- **A point mass** at $x_0$ — assigns 1 to any set containing $x_0$, and 0 otherwise.

The point of the abstraction: probability and volume are the same *kind* of object, so you can meaningfully compare them, and that comparison is what a density is.

## 7. Density = an exchange rate between two measures

Here is the concept the whole paper rests on.

A **density is never absolute — it is always relative to some reference measure.** A density is the local exchange rate between two ways of measuring size.

The ordinary probability density function $q(x)$ you already know is the exchange rate between probability and volume:

$$Q(A) = \int_A q(x)\,\underbrace{dx}_{\text{volume}}$$

Read $q(x)$ as: *"near the point $x$, one unit of volume is worth $q(x)$ units of probability."* Where $q$ is large, a small box holds a lot of probability. This is called the **Lebesgue density**, and "Lebesgue" names the denominator of the exchange rate — volume.

Now the leap: **nothing forces the reference to be volume.** You can ask for the exchange rate between one probability measure and another. If $P$ and $Q$ are both probability measures, a function $R$ satisfying

$$P(A) = \int_A R(x)\,\underbrace{dQ(x)}_{\text{probability under } Q}$$

is the density of $P$ *with respect to $Q$*. It answers: *"near $x$, is $P$ putting more or less mass than $Q$ does, and by what factor?"* $R(x) = 2$ means $P$ is twice as concentrated there as $Q$; $R(x) = 0.5$ means half.

This is exactly the "lens" from Section 4, now with a name. **The correction $\rho_c$ is a density with respect to the Gaussian, rather than with respect to volume.**

Two immediate consequences worth internalizing:

- $\rho_c$ does **not** integrate to 1 against $dx$. It integrates to 1 against $dQ_c$. These are different statements and confusing them is the most common error here.
- $\rho_c$ is dimensionless — it's a ratio of two mass-assignments. It's a *comparison*, not a *quantity*.

## 8. Absolute continuity: when the exchange rate exists

Exchange rates can fail to exist. Suppose $Q$ assigns zero mass to a set $A$, but $P$ assigns positive mass to it. Then you'd need

$$\underbrace{P(A)}_{>0} = \int_A R \,dQ = 0 \quad \text{(since } Q(A)=0\text{, the integral is 0 regardless of } R\text{)}$$

Contradiction. No function $R$ can fix this. You cannot express "there is mass here" as a rescaling of "there is no mass here." Multiplying zero by anything finite is still zero.

The condition ruling this out is **absolute continuity**, written $P \ll Q$:

$$Q(A) = 0 \implies P(A) = 0 \quad\text{for every measurable } A$$

*"$Q$ must have mass everywhere $P$ does."* Intuitively: **the reference must cover the target.** The lens can dim or brighten light, but it cannot create light where the spotlight shines none.

### Why this makes the Gaussian reference an excellent default

A Gaussian has **full support**: $q_c(x) > 0$ at every single point of $\mathbb{R}^k$, however far out. It's astronomically small in the tails, but never zero. Therefore $P_c \ll Q_c$ holds automatically for *any* target distribution that has an ordinary density.

This is a genuinely strong statement and it's the paper's §11.2: with a Gaussian reference, the correction $\rho_c$ *always exists*, no matter what shape the true cluster has. Whether a *degree-$m$ polynomial* can represent it well is a separate question about approximation, but the object we're trying to approximate is guaranteed to be there.

Contrast with a bounded reference — say uniform on $[0,1]$. It assigns zero mass outside $[0,1]$, so it cannot represent any cluster with mass at $x = 1.5$, ever, for any correction. This is the trap Part X warns about.

## 9. The Radon–Nikodym theorem

The theorem that makes it official:

> **Radon–Nikodym.** If $P \ll Q$ (and both are $\sigma$-finite), then there exists an essentially unique non-negative function $R$ with $P(A) = \int_A R\,dQ$ for all $A$. It's written $R = \frac{dP}{dQ}$ and called the Radon–Nikodym derivative.

The derivative notation is apt. An ordinary derivative $\frac{df}{dx}$ is a local rate of change of $f$ per unit $x$. The Radon–Nikodym derivative $\frac{dP}{dQ}$ is a local rate of $P$-mass per unit $Q$-mass. Same idea, different currencies.

What the theorem buys us: **existence without construction.** It says the object we want is guaranteed to be there, and it's unique. That licenses us to go looking for it — to set up an approximation and optimize — knowing we're chasing something real, not fitting a fiction.

## 10. Chaining the two densities together

Now assemble the argument from §2 of the paper.

We have two facts:

$$dP_c = R_c\, dQ_c \quad \text{(Radon–Nikodym: } P_c \text{ relative to the Gaussian)}$$
$$dQ_c = q_c\, dx \quad \text{(the Gaussian has an ordinary pdf)}$$

Substitute:

$$dP_c = R_c \cdot q_c\, dx$$

So $P_c$ has a Lebesgue density too, and it is

$$\boxed{p_c(x) = q_c(x)\,R_c(x)}$$

This is a **chain rule for measures**: to convert probability-to-probability into probability-to-volume, compose the exchange rates, exactly as you'd multiply currency conversion factors.

This step is what converts an abstract measure-theoretic statement into a usable model. We need an actual function of $x$ to plug into a log-likelihood; this delivers it.

### The reframing, and why it's the real payoff

Compare two ways of stating the modeling problem:

| Framing | Task |
|---|---|
| Direct | Learn the density $p_c$ of this cluster from scratch |
| Density-ratio | Learn how this cluster *differs* from a Gaussian |

The second is dramatically easier, for a reason worth stating plainly: **you've off-loaded all the hard, global structure onto the reference.** The Gaussian already handles normalization, positivity, decay at infinity, location, scale, and orientation. What's left for $R_c$ to express is only the *residual* shape — and if the cluster is roughly Gaussian, that residual is close to the constant function 1, which is easy to approximate with a few terms.

This is the same logic as fitting residuals in regression, or using a good proposal distribution in importance sampling: **choose the reference so that what remains is simple.** The paper states this as its guiding principle in eq. (52).

# Part IV — Local Coordinates and Whitening

## 11. The problem: each cluster lives in its own frame

We want to express $\rho_c$ in some basis of functions. But cluster 1 might sit near the origin with unit scale, while cluster 2 sits at $(1000, -50)$ stretched along a diagonal. A basis of functions that resolves detail at the right scale for one is useless for the other.

The fix: give every cluster its own coordinate system, in which it looks standard.

## 12. The whitening transform

$$u_c(x) = \Sigma_c^{-1/2}(x-\mu_c)$$

Two operations:

1. **Subtract $\mu_c$** — translate so the cluster's center is at the origin.
2. **Multiply by $\Sigma_c^{-1/2}$** — undo the stretching and rotation.

**The matrix square root.** $\Sigma^{1/2}$ means a matrix $A$ with $AA^\top = \Sigma$ — the matrix analogue of an ordinary square root. Then $\Sigma^{-1/2} = (\Sigma^{1/2})^{-1}$. Note this is *not* elementwise: $\Sigma^{-1/2}_{ij} \ne 1/\sqrt{\Sigma_{ij}}$.

It's computed via eigendecomposition. Write $\Sigma = V\Lambda V^\top$ with $V$ orthogonal (the eigenvectors, i.e. the ellipsoid's principal axes) and $\Lambda$ diagonal with positive entries (the eigenvalues, i.e. the squared axis lengths). Then

$$\Sigma^{-1/2} = V\Lambda^{-1/2}V^\top$$

where $\Lambda^{-1/2}$ *is* elementwise on the diagonal. The recipe reads left-to-right on a vector: **rotate into the ellipsoid's own axes, divide each axis by its length, rotate back.**

This is exactly why the model requires $\Sigma_c \succ 0$ (positive definite). Positive definiteness means all eigenvalues $\lambda_j > 0$, so $\lambda_j^{-1/2}$ is real and finite. A zero eigenvalue would mean the cluster is flat in some direction — infinitely thin — and the whitening transform would blow up. It's also why §8.3 adds a ridge $\eta I_k$ to the covariance update: cheap insurance against a component collapsing onto a lower-dimensional subspace.

## 13. What whitening achieves

Recall from Section 12: $u_c = u_c(x) = \Sigma_c^{-1/2}(x-\mu_c)$ — the whitening transform applied to $x$, i.e. $x$ re-expressed in cluster $c$'s own local, standardized coordinates.

If $x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_c,\Sigma_c)$, then

$$\mathbb{E}[u_c] = 0, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(u_c) = \Sigma_c^{-1/2}\,\Sigma_c\,\Sigma_c^{-1/2\top} = I_k$$

So $u_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_k)$: **the standard Gaussian**. Any ellipsoidal cloud, wherever it sits and however it's tilted, becomes a unit sphere of points at the origin. The name "whitening" is by analogy with white noise — equal power in all directions, no preferred axis.

### The distance the whitened coordinate measures

There's a second, equally important way to read $u_c(x)$: not just as a coordinate, but as a *distance*. Working out $\|u_c(x)\|^2$ explicitly:

$$\|u_c(x)\|^2 = u_c(x)^\top u_c(x) = (\Sigma_c^{-1/2}(x-\mu_c))^\top(\Sigma_c^{-1/2}(x-\mu_c)) = (x-\mu_c)^\top\Sigma_c^{-1}(x-\mu_c)$$

(the two $\Sigma_c^{-1/2}$ factors combine to $\Sigma_c^{-1}$, regardless of which square-root convention from Section 14 you used). This quantity has a name: the **Mahalanobis distance** (squared) from $x$ to $\mu_c$.

**Why an ordinary ruler is the wrong tool here.** Plain Euclidean distance, $\|x-\mu_c\|$, treats every direction as equally spread out. But an elliptical cluster isn't — it's wide along its long axis and narrow along its short one. A point sitting 3 units away along the *long* axis is well within the cluster's typical scatter; a point 3 units away along the *short* axis is way out in the sparse tail. Euclidean distance can't tell these apart. Mahalanobis distance is the fix: it re-measures distance in units of "how many standard deviations of the cluster's own spread," separately in each direction, so a point is only counted as "far" if it's far *relative to how much the cluster naturally varies in that direction.*

**Made concrete, reusing the $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}$ example from Section 2.** Recall that ellipse stretches along the 45° direction (variance 8, so spread of $\sqrt8\approx2.83$) and is narrow along 135° (variance 2, spread of $\sqrt2\approx1.41$). Take two points, one on each axis:

| point $x$ | direction | raw Euclidean distance | Mahalanobis distance |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(2,\ 2)$ | long axis (45°) | $2.83$ | $1.00$ |
| $(1,\,-1)$ | short axis (135°) | $1.41$ | $1.00$ |

By an ordinary ruler these look completely different — one point is twice as far away as the other. But **both sit at exactly one Mahalanobis unit from the center**: each is exactly one standard deviation out, *measured along its own direction*. That's the sense in which Mahalanobis distance is the "honest" distance for an elliptical cloud — it already accounts for the fact that the cluster tolerates more spread in some directions than others.

And this is precisely what whitening computes. Applying $u_c$ to each point above:

$$u_c((2,2)) = (0.707,\ 0.707), \qquad u_c((1,-1)) = (0.707,\ -0.707)$$

Both have length exactly 1. Whitening didn't just decorrelate the cloud — it rescaled every direction so that **Mahalanobis distance in the original coordinates becomes ordinary Euclidean distance in the whitened ones.** The ellipse literally becomes the unit circle, and "one Mahalanobis unit out" literally becomes "distance 1 from the origin."

**The connection to the Gaussian.** Look again at the density formula from Section 2: $q_c(x) \propto \exp\!\left(-\tfrac12(x-\mu_c)^\top\Sigma_c^{-1}(x-\mu_c)\right)$. The exponent *is* $-\tfrac12\|u_c(x)\|^2$ — the negative half of the squared Mahalanobis distance. So a Gaussian was never really a function of raw position; it only ever cared how many Mahalanobis units out you were. $u_c$ isn't a new idea bolted onto the model — it's making explicit, as literal coordinates, the exact distance the Gaussian was silently using all along to decide how much density to assign.

### Why the model can't work without it

The Hermite basis (Part V) is orthonormal against the **standard** Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0,I_k)$ — with the identity matrix, not a general $\Sigma$. Orthonormality is what makes the normalization constant collapse to a simple sum of squared coefficients, satisfying design constraint 2.

**Made concrete with a small example** (the full mechanism is Section 22; this is a preview with actual numbers). Take just the first two 1-D basis functions, $\phi_0(u)=1$ and $\phi_1(u)=u$, and build $g(u) = 2\cdot\phi_0(u) + 3\cdot\phi_1(u) = 2+3u$. Ask: what is $\mathbb{E}[g(u)^2]$ when $u$ is drawn from the *standard* normal $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$?

$$\mathbb{E}[(2+3u)^2] = 4\,\mathbb{E}[1] + 12\,\mathbb{E}[u] + 9\,\mathbb{E}[u^2] = 4(1) + 12(0) + 9(1) = 4+9 = 13$$

The cross term vanished because $\mathbb{E}[u]=0$ (that's $\phi_0\perp\phi_1$), and the $u^2$ term simplified to exactly $9$ because $\mathbb{E}[u^2]=1$ (that's $\phi_1$ having unit length). The result, $13 = 2^2+3^2$, is exactly sum-of-coefficients-squared — no integral needed once you know it's the standard normal. Numerically confirmed by direct integration: $13.0$, exactly.

**Now the contrast, showing why "standard" specifically matters.** Suppose instead $u$ had come from an *un-whitened* Gaussian with variance 4 (i.e. $\Sigma = 4$, not $\Sigma=1$) — this is what you'd be stuck with if you skipped whitening and worked directly in raw coordinates. The cross term still vanishes (mean is still 0), but now $\mathbb{E}[u^2] = 4$, not $1$:

$$\mathbb{E}[(2+3u)^2] = 4(1) + 12(0) + 9(4) = 4+36 = 40 \;\ne\; 13 = 2^2+3^2$$

Confirmed by integration: exactly $40.0$. The clean formula breaks — you'd instead get $b_0^2 + \sigma^2 b_1^2$, a quantity that depends on $\Sigma_c$ and would have to be recomputed differently for every component and every EM iteration as $\Sigma_c$ updates. **That's the whole reason whitening has to happen first**: it's what forces every component's basis functions to be evaluated against variance exactly 1 in every direction, so the same simple sum-of-squares formula applies universally, rather than a different $\Sigma_c$-dependent formula for every cluster.

So: whitening is what lets us use a *fixed, universal* basis for every cluster in every dataset, rather than deriving a bespoke orthonormal system for each $\Sigma_c$ at every EM iteration. It converts an intractable per-component problem into a change of variables.

## 14. A subtlety: the square root isn't unique

Any $A$ with $AA^\top = \Sigma$ works, and there are infinitely many (as before, $\Sigma$ here is the covariance matrix — the same object as $\Sigma_c$ from Sections 2 and 12, just with the component subscript dropped since this holds for any one component's covariance). Two are standard:

| Method | Symmetric? | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eigendecomposition $V\Lambda^{-1/2}V^\top$ | Yes | $O(k^3)$, larger constant | The canonical choice |
| Inverse Cholesky factor $L^{-1}$ | No | $O(k^3)$, cheapest | Triangular, very stable |

Any two differ by an orthogonal (rotation/reflection) factor: $A_2 = A_1 Q$ with $QQ^\top = I$.

**For a plain GMM this is invisible**, because the Gaussian density depends on $u$ only through $\|u\|^2$, which rotations preserve. **For the Hermite-GMM it's a real question**, because the basis functions $\phi_\alpha(u) = \prod_j \psi_{\alpha_j}(u_j)$ depend on individual coordinates and are not rotation-invariant. Different square roots give different coefficient vectors $b_c$ — here's exactly why that matters in practice.

**Why this is actually a problem, made concrete.** Reuse $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}$ from Section 2, whose long axis points along $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ at 45°. Building both whitening matrices:

$$A_{\text{sym}} = \begin{pmatrix}0.530 & -0.177\\-0.177 & 0.530\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_{\text{chol}} = \begin{pmatrix}0.447 & 0\\-0.335 & 0.559\end{pmatrix}$$

Both are legitimate — each satisfies $A\Sigma A^\top = I$. But take the same physical correction: "add skew *along the ellipse's long axis*," i.e. a linear function $h(x) = c^\top x$ with $c=(1,1)/\sqrt2$ pointing along that axis. Expressed in each whitened coordinate system, this one physical effect needs a completely different coefficient vector $w$:

$$w_{\text{sym}} = (2.00,\ 2.00), \qquad w_{\text{chol}} = (2.53,\ 1.26)$$

Both are correct — plugging either into $w^\top u(x)$ and evaluating at any test point reproduces the identical $h(x)=c^\top x$ exactly (verified numerically to four decimal places on random points). **But if you fit $b_c$ under one convention and try to read off "how much skew is along the long axis" by inspecting a single coefficient, you'll get a different number — and a different pattern of which coefficients are even nonzero — than someone who fit the identical model under the other convention.** That's the concrete failure mode: individual coefficients are not comparable, interpretable in isolation, or stable across implementations, runs, or even library versions, even though the fitted *density* $\rho_c$ is exactly the same object in both cases.

The resolution is reassuring. Rotations preserve total polynomial degree, and because the standard Gaussian measure is itself rotation-invariant, each degree-$d$ Hermite block maps into itself under rotation. Verified numerically in $k=2$: rotating a degree-3 basis function redistributes its expansion entirely within the degree-3 block (99.9% of the squared coefficient mass), preserving norm.

Consequences:

- The **model class** is the same whichever square root you use.
- The **regularizer** $w_\alpha = |\alpha|^2$, depending only on total degree, is likewise invariant.
- **Individual coefficients** are not comparable across implementations; **degree-block energies** $\sum_{|\alpha|=d} b_{c,\alpha}^2$ are, and those are the interpretable quantities anyway.

*Practical warning:* use one convention consistently across EM iterations. Eigendecomposition routines can flip eigenvector signs or reorder near-degenerate eigenvalues between calls, silently rotating your frame mid-optimization and scrambling the $b_c$ you're carrying forward. Cholesky is deterministic and cheaper — the safer default.

# Part V — Function Spaces and Orthonormal Bases

We need to build $\rho_c$ out of something. This part explains what "expand in a basis" means for functions, and why Hermite polynomials are the uniquely right choice here.

## 15. Functions as vectors

The organizing analogy: **a function is a vector with infinitely many components.**

A vector in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a list of 3 numbers indexed by $\{1,2,3\}$. A function $f$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is a list of numbers indexed by $\mathbb{R}$ — the value $f(t)$ at each $t$. Same object, bigger index set.

Once you accept that, everything from linear algebra transfers, provided you can define an inner product. For vectors it's $\langle a,b\rangle = \sum_j a_j b_j$. For functions, replace the sum with an integral — but *weighted by a measure*:

$$\langle f,g\rangle_\mu = \int f(t)g(t)\,d\mu(t)$$

**The measure is part of the geometry.** It says which regions of the domain "count" and how much. Change the measure and you change what orthogonality means — a fact we'll use decisively.

The space of functions with finite $\langle f,f\rangle_\mu$ is written $L^2(\mu)$: square-integrable functions. It's a Hilbert space — an infinite-dimensional vector space with a working notion of length and angle.

## 16. Orthonormal bases and why they're worth having

A set $\{e_1,e_2,\dots\}$ is **orthonormal** if

$$\langle e_i, e_j\rangle = \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases}1 & i=j\\ 0 & i \ne j\end{cases}$$

Each has unit length; distinct ones are perpendicular. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ this is $\hat x, \hat y, \hat z$.

The reason to want one: **coefficients decouple.** Write $f = \sum_j c_j e_j$. To find $c_j$, take the inner product with $e_j$; every other term dies by orthogonality, leaving

$$c_j = \langle f, e_j\rangle$$

You get each coefficient independently, without solving a system. Compare a non-orthogonal basis, where finding coefficients requires inverting a Gram matrix — expensive, and unstable when the basis functions are nearly parallel (as monomials $1, t, t^2, t^3$ notoriously are).

### Parseval's identity — the load-bearing fact

For an orthonormal basis, the squared length of a function equals the sum of squared coefficients:

$$\|f\|^2 = \langle f,f\rangle = \langle \sum_i c_ie_i, \sum_j c_je_j\rangle = \sum_{i,j}c_ic_j\underbrace{\langle e_i,e_j\rangle}_{\delta_{ij}} = \sum_j c_j^2$$

**This is Pythagoras in infinite dimensions**, and it is the single fact that makes the entire model tractable. Look at what it says: an *integral* on the left, a *finite sum of squared coefficients* on the right. An integral you'd have to compute numerically becomes arithmetic on the parameters you already have.

Keep this in view — Section 22 is nothing but a direct application of it.

## 17. Hermite polynomials: the right basis for Gaussian measure

The familiar orthonormal basis is Fourier — sines and cosines, orthonormal under *uniform* measure on an interval. But our measure is Gaussian, not uniform.

**Why the measure matters this much — concretely.** Recall from Section 15 that the inner product is *weighted by a measure*: $\langle f,g\rangle_\mu = \int fg\,d\mu$. Under a *uniform* measure on, say, $[-10,10]$, every point contributes equally — a stretch of the domain near $\pm10$ counts exactly as much toward the integral as a stretch near $0$. Under the *Gaussian* measure, that's dramatically false. The density itself decays fast:

| distance from center $u$ | Gaussian density $\phi(u)$ |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.399 |
| 1 | 0.242 |
| 2 | 0.054 |
| 3 | 0.0044 |
| 5 | 0.0000015 |

By $u=5$, the density is six orders of magnitude smaller than at the center. The probability mass living beyond $|u|>3$ is only about $0.27\%$ of the total — compare a uniform measure on $[-10,10]$, where the region beyond $|u|>3$ still holds $70\%$ of the mass. **So under a Gaussian, whatever two functions do far from the origin contributes almost nothing to their inner product — only their behavior near the center really counts.** That's "distant regions barely count": it's the density's own exponential decay, downweighting the tails almost to nothing in any integral computed against it.

**This isn't a minor detail — it actually breaks Fourier's orthogonality.** $\sin(x)$ and $\sin(2x)$ are textbook-orthogonal under the uniform measure on $[-\pi,\pi]$: $\langle\sin x,\sin 2x\rangle = 0$ exactly, confirmed numerically. But integrate that *same pair* of functions against the standard Gaussian measure over all of $\mathbb{R}$ instead, and

$$\langle\sin x,\sin 2x\rangle_{\gamma} = 0.298 \;\ne\; 0$$

Not orthogonal at all. Other Fourier pairs fare similarly: $\langle\sin x,\sin3x\rangle_\gamma = 0.068$, $\langle\sin2x,\sin3x\rangle_\gamma = 0.303$ — none of them vanish. **Orthogonality is a property of a pair of functions *and* a measure together, not of the functions alone.** Fourier modes are the right basis for the measure they were built for (uniform); switch the measure to Gaussian and that same basis simply isn't orthogonal anymore. We need a basis orthonormal under $\gamma_k$, the standard Gaussian measure:

$$d\gamma_k(u) = (2\pi)^{-k/2}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\|u\|^2}{2}\right)du$$

The answer is the **probabilists' Hermite polynomials** $\mathrm{He}_n$. They're what you get by running **Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization** on $1, t, t^2, t^3, \dots$ using the Gaussian-weighted inner product.

**What Gram–Schmidt is, generically.** Recall Section 15: functions are vectors, and $\langle f,g\rangle_\mu = \int fg\,d\mu$ is their inner product. Gram–Schmidt is the standard recipe for turning *any* list of vectors into an orthonormal one, one at a time:

1. Take the next vector in your list.
2. Subtract off its projection onto every vector you've already orthogonalized — this removes exactly the part that overlaps with what you already have.
3. What's left is, by construction, orthogonal to everything before it. Normalize it to unit length, and move to the next vector.

The projection of $v$ onto an already-orthogonalized $u$ is the familiar formula $\dfrac{\langle v,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}u$ — "how much of $v$ points along $u$," scaled appropriately.

**Run explicitly, using the Gaussian-weighted inner product $\langle f,g\rangle = \mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[f(Z)g(Z)]$, starting from $1,t,t^2,t^3,\dots$:**

*Step 0:* $\mathrm{He}_0 = 1$. Nothing to subtract — it's first in line. (And it's already unit length: $\mathbb{E}[1^2]=1$.)

*Step 1:* Take $t$. Project onto $\mathrm{He}_0=1$: the coefficient is $\langle t,1\rangle/\langle1,1\rangle = \mathbb{E}[t]/1 = 0$, since a standard normal has mean zero. Nothing to subtract — $t$ was already orthogonal to the constant. So $\mathrm{He}_1 = t$.

*Step 2:* Take $t^2$. Project onto $\mathrm{He}_0=1$: coefficient $= \langle t^2,1\rangle/\langle1,1\rangle = \mathbb{E}[t^2]/1 = \mathbb{E}[t^2]$. Here's why that equals $1$: variance is *defined* as

$$\mathrm{Var}(Z) = \mathbb{E}[Z^2] - (\mathbb{E}[Z])^2$$

Step 1 already established $\mathbb{E}[t]=0$ for the standard normal, so the second term drops out and $\mathrm{Var}(t) = \mathbb{E}[t^2]$ — variance and $\mathbb{E}[t^2]$ are the *same number* whenever the mean is zero. And the standard normal $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ has variance exactly $1$ by definition (it's built into the "1" in $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$). So $\mathbb{E}[t^2] = 1$. Project onto $\mathrm{He}_1=t$: coefficient $=\langle t^2,t\rangle/\langle t,t\rangle = \mathbb{E}[t^3]/\mathbb{E}[t^2] = 0/1=0$ (odd moments of a symmetric distribution vanish). Subtracting only the first: $\mathrm{He}_2 = t^2 - 1\cdot 1 - 0\cdot t = t^2-1$. Confirmed numerically: $\langle t^2,1\rangle = 1.0$ exactly, giving $t^2-1$, and $\langle \mathrm{He}_2,\mathrm{He}_2\rangle = 2.0$ (which is why $\psi_2 = \mathrm{He}_2/\sqrt2$ below).

*Step 3:* Take $t^3$. Its projections onto $\mathrm{He}_0=1$ and $\mathrm{He}_2=t^2-1$ are both zero (odd function against even ones — $\mathbb{E}[t^3]=0$, $\mathbb{E}[t^5-t^3]=0$). Its projection onto $\mathrm{He}_1=t$: coefficient $=\langle t^3,t\rangle/\langle t,t\rangle = \mathbb{E}[t^4]/\mathbb{E}[t^2] = 3/1=3$. Subtracting: $\mathrm{He}_3 = t^3 - 3t$. Confirmed numerically — projection coefficient exactly $3.0$, resulting $\langle\mathrm{He}_3,\mathrm{He}_3\rangle=6.0$ (hence $\psi_3=\mathrm{He}_3/\sqrt6$).

So each $\mathrm{He}_n$ is genuinely *constructed*, not just pattern-matched: take the next monomial, strip out every lower-order piece it shares with what came before, and what remains is a new direction, orthogonal to all the rest.

$$\mathrm{He}_0 = 1,\quad \mathrm{He}_1 = t,\quad \mathrm{He}_2 = t^2-1,\quad \mathrm{He}_3 = t^3-3t,\quad \mathrm{He}_4 = t^4-6t^2+3$$

Notice $\mathrm{He}_2 = t^2 - 1$: it's $t^2$ with its Gaussian mean subtracted off, so it's orthogonal to the constant. Every Hermite polynomial is the corresponding monomial with all lower-degree Gaussian structure removed.

They satisfy

$$\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[\mathrm{He}_m(Z)\mathrm{He}_n(Z)] = n!\,\delta_{mn}$$

Orthogonal, but not normalized — hence dividing by $\sqrt{n!}$:

$$\psi_n(t) = \frac{\mathrm{He}_n(t)}{\sqrt{n!}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbb{E}[\psi_m\psi_n] = \delta_{mn}$$

Explicitly, the first five:

$$\psi_0 = 1,\quad \psi_1 = t,\quad \psi_2 = \tfrac{t^2-1}{\sqrt2},\quad \psi_3 = \tfrac{t^3-3t}{\sqrt6},\quad \psi_4 = \tfrac{t^4-6t^2+3}{\sqrt{24}}$$

**Slogan: Hermite polynomials are to the Gaussian what Fourier modes are to the uniform.** Each basis is adapted to a measure; use the one matching your reference.

They also have a satisfying probabilistic meaning: $\mathbb{E}[\psi_n(Z)]$ for $n \ge 1$ is zero, and $\psi_n$ picks out exactly the $n$-th order shape information that lower degrees don't already capture. Degree 3 measures skewness *after* accounting for mean and variance. This "orthogonalized moments" reading is why the degree interpretation in Part VII is clean.

## 18. Multiple dimensions: tensor products and multi-indices

In $\mathbb{R}^k$, the standard Gaussian factorizes — its coordinates are independent:

$$\gamma_k = \gamma_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \gamma_1$$

(This is another gift from whitening. A general $\Sigma$ has correlated coordinates and does *not* factorize.)

**What "factorizes" means, concretely.** A joint density "factorizes" if it splits into a separate product, one factor per coordinate, each depending on *only that one coordinate*: $p(u_1,\dots,u_k) = f_1(u_1)\cdots f_k(u_k)$. This is exactly the statement that the coordinates are statistically independent — knowing $u_1$ tells you nothing about the distribution of $u_2$. The $\otimes$ above just denotes this "combine independent measures into one joint measure" operation.

Check it directly for the standard 2-D Gaussian. Its density is $\gamma_2(u_1,u_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{u_1^2+u_2^2}{2}\right)$. Because the exponent is a plain *sum* $u_1^2+u_2^2$ with no cross term, the exponential splits cleanly:

$$\gamma_2(u_1,u_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-u_1^2/2}e^{-u_2^2/2} = \underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u_1^2/2}\right)}_{\gamma_1(u_1)}\cdot\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-u_2^2/2}\right)}_{\gamma_1(u_2)}$$

Verified numerically at several points, e.g. $u=(0.5,-1.2)$: joint density $=0.068366$, product of the two 1-D densities $=0.068366$ — identical.

**Why a general covariance breaks this** (making good on the parenthetical above). Reuse $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}$ from Section 2. Its inverse has a nonzero off-diagonal entry, $\Sigma^{-1}_{12}=-0.1875$, so the exponent $-\tfrac12 x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x$ contains a genuine cross term proportional to $x_1x_2$ that cannot be pulled apart into "a function of $x_1$" times "a function of $x_2$" — $e^{ax_1^2+bx_2^2+cx_1x_2}$ only splits into separate factors in $x_1$ and $x_2$ when $c=0$. A direct separability check confirms it: for a genuinely factorizing density, $p(x_1,x_2)\cdot p(0,0)$ must equal $p(x_1,0)\cdot p(0,x_2)$ for every point. For this correlated Gaussian at $(x_1,x_2)=(1,1)$: $0.001397$ versus $0.001158$ — not equal. **The correlated Gaussian genuinely does not factorize**, confirming why whitening — which strips out exactly that correlation (Sections 12–14) — is what's needed to reach the factorizing $I_k$ used here.

This is also why the derivation two paragraphs below works at all: splitting a joint expectation $\mathbb{E}[\phi_\alpha(U)\phi_\beta(U)]$ into a *product* of one-dimensional expectations is only mathematically valid when the coordinates of $U$ are independent — i.e., precisely when the underlying measure factorizes.

Because it factorizes, products of 1-D basis functions form a basis for the joint space. Index them by a **multi-index** $\alpha = (\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_k)$, a list of degrees, one per coordinate:

$$\phi_\alpha(u) = \prod_{j=1}^{k}\psi_{\alpha_j}(u_j)$$

Orthonormality survives, because the expectation of a product of independent factors is the product of expectations:

$$\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_k)}[\phi_\alpha(U)\phi_\beta(U)] = \prod_{j=1}^k \mathbb{E}[\psi_{\alpha_j}(U_j)\psi_{\beta_j}(U_j)] = \prod_j \delta_{\alpha_j\beta_j} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}$$

Concretely in $k=2$: $\phi_{(3,0)}(u) = \psi_3(u_1)$ is skewness along axis 1 alone. $\phi_{(1,2)}(u) = \psi_1(u_1)\psi_2(u_2) = u_1\cdot\frac{u_2^2-1}{\sqrt2}$ is an *interaction* — the spread along axis 2 changing as you move along axis 1, i.e. a fan or wedge shape. Mixed multi-indices are where genuinely multivariate structure lives.

## 19. Truncation: why total degree, and the cost of dimension

Infinitely many basis functions is not a model. Truncate by **total degree**:

$$\mathcal{A}_m = \{\alpha : |\alpha| \le m\}, \qquad |\alpha| = \alpha_1+\cdots+\alpha_k$$

Total degree (rather than, say, capping each coordinate separately) is the right cut for two reasons: it's the natural notion of "complexity" for polynomials, and — as Section 14 showed — it's rotation-invariant, so the model class doesn't depend on arbitrary coordinate choices.

The count is a stars-and-bars combinatorial identity:

$$|\mathcal{A}_m| = \binom{k+m}{m}$$

This grows brutally:

| $k \backslash m$ | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 6 | 10 | 15 | 21 |
| 5 | 21 | 56 | 126 | 252 |
| 10 | 66 | 286 | 1001 | 3003 |
| 20 | 231 | 1771 | 10626 | 53130 |

At $k=10, m=4$: **1001 coefficients per component.** With $C=5$ components that's 5005 correction parameters on top of the Gaussian ones. This is the curse of dimensionality in its plainest form, and it is the model's principal practical limitation. It's why Part IX's advice on regularization and dropping low degrees isn't optional garnish — it's load-bearing.

# Part VI — Making the Correction Valid

We have a way to build flexible functions: $g_c(u) = \sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_m} b_{c,\alpha}\phi_\alpha(u)$. But Section 5 demanded non-negativity and exact normalization. A polynomial gives neither for free.

## 20. Attempt 1: additive correction — fails

The obvious try is $\rho_c(u) = 1 + \sum_{\alpha\ne 0} a_\alpha\phi_\alpha(u)$.

**Normalization: fine.** Every $\phi_\alpha$ with $\alpha \ne 0$ is orthogonal to $\phi_0 = 1$, so $\mathbb{E}[\phi_\alpha] = 0$, and the mean is 1 automatically.

**Positivity: fails.** A non-constant polynomial of odd degree goes to $-\infty$ in one direction. Even for even degree, nothing prevents it dipping below zero somewhere. And a negative density is not merely inelegant — $\log \rho_c$ is undefined, the log-likelihood is undefined, and the optimizer receives NaN.

You could constrain the coefficients to keep $\rho_c \ge 0$, but the set of coefficient vectors making a multivariate polynomial globally non-negative is a horrible non-convex set with no usable description. Dead end.

*(This construction is the classical Gram–Charlier / Edgeworth expansion, and negative-density regions are its well-known chronic failure.)*

## 21. Attempt 2: exponential — right, but computationally fatal

Try $\rho_c(u) \propto \exp(\sum_\alpha b_\alpha \phi_\alpha(u))$. Positivity is automatic — exponentials are positive. This is an exponential family, and theoretically it's the elegant choice.

But normalizing requires

$$Z(b) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^k} \exp(\sum_\alpha b_\alpha\phi_\alpha(u))d\gamma_k(u)$$

and this integral has **no closed form**. It must be recomputed by numerical quadrature or MCMC at *every* gradient step, for *every* component, at *every* EM iteration. Worse, for polynomials of odd degree it can diverge outright.

This violates design constraint 2 decisively. The exponential is the theoretician's answer; it is not the practitioner's.

## 22. Attempt 3: square it — the trick that works

$$\rho_c(u) = \frac{g_c(u)^2 + \varepsilon}{B_c + \varepsilon}, \qquad B_c = \sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_m}b_{c,\alpha}^2$$

**Positivity: automatic.** A real square is non-negative, no constraints on $b_c$ whatsoever. The optimizer roams freely over all of $\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}_m|}$.

**Normalization: free — and here is why.** Expand the square and use orthonormality:

$$\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_k)}[g_c(U)^2] = \sum_{\alpha,\beta}b_{c,\alpha}b_{c,\beta}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\phi_\alpha\phi_\beta]}_{\delta_{\alpha\beta}} = \sum_\alpha b_{c,\alpha}^2 = B_c$$

**This is Parseval from Section 16, doing exactly the job it was introduced for.** The integral we feared collapses into the sum of squared coefficients — a quantity we can compute in $|\mathcal{A}_m|$ multiplications. Add the constant $\varepsilon$ (which integrates to $\varepsilon$ since $\gamma_k$ is a probability measure), divide by $B_c + \varepsilon$, and:

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^k}\rho_c(u)\,d\gamma_k(u) = \frac{B_c + \varepsilon}{B_c+\varepsilon} = 1 \qquad\text{exactly, for every } b_c$$

Design constraint 2, satisfied. **The whole chain — whiten so the reference is standard, use the basis orthonormal under that reference, square to force positivity, normalize by Parseval — exists to make this one line true.** Every earlier choice was in service of it.

**Expressiveness:** squaring costs less than you'd fear. $g_c^2$ ranges over squares of degree-$m$ polynomials, a rich class; and since any non-negative function can be approximated by the square of something (roughly, take a square root), you're not badly restricted. But the map $b \mapsto \rho$ is now *non-injective and non-monotone* — a real complication we quantify in Part XII.

## 23. What $\varepsilon$ is for

Since $g_c$ is a polynomial, it has roots. At any root, $g_c(u)^2 = 0$, so without $\varepsilon$ the density would be exactly zero at that point. If a data point lands near such a **node**, $\log\rho_c \to -\infty$ and the log-likelihood explodes.

$\varepsilon$ floors the density at $\varepsilon/(B_c+\varepsilon) > 0$. It's a numerical safety catch — the same role as the ridge $\eta I_k$ on covariances.

Tuning: too small and tail points produce near-infinite gradients; too large and $\rho_c$ is flattened toward the constant 1, washing out the correction. A scale-aware choice like $\varepsilon \approx 10^{-3}\,\|b_c\|^2$ keeps its influence proportionate as the coefficients grow.

# Part VII — The Assembled Model

## 24. The full specification

$$p_\theta(x) = \sum_{c=1}^{C}\pi_c\,\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_c,\Sigma_c)\;\frac{(\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_m}b_{c,\alpha}\,\phi_\alpha(\Sigma_c^{-1/2}(x-\mu_c)))^2+\varepsilon}{\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_m}b_{c,\alpha}^2+\varepsilon}$$

Parameters: $\theta = \{\pi_c,\mu_c,\Sigma_c,b_c\}_{c=1}^C$, subject to $\pi_c\ge 0$, $\sum_c\pi_c=1$, $\Sigma_c\succ 0$.

Reading it in one pass, from the inside out:

1. $x-\mu_c$ — center on cluster $c$.
2. $\Sigma_c^{-1/2}(\cdot)$ — whiten, so the cluster's own scale and orientation are unit.
3. $\phi_\alpha(u)$ — evaluate the Hermite basis in those coordinates.

   *What this step actually produces.* "The Hermite basis" isn't one function — it's the whole indexed family $\{\phi_\alpha : \alpha\in\mathcal{A}_m\}$ from Section 19, one basis function per multi-index in the truncated set. So this step means: plug the *same* whitened vector $u$ from step 2 into *every one* of these basis functions, producing a whole list of numbers — one per $\alpha$ — not a single value. Step 4 is what collapses that list back down to one number.

   *Concretely,* reuse $u=(0.7071,0.7071)$ — this is exactly $u_c(x)$ computed in Section 13 for $x=(2,2)$ under $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}$. Take $k=2$ (two dimensions) and $m=2$ (corrections up to total degree 2).

   *What the index set is.* $\mathcal{A}_m$ was defined in Section 19: it's the list of multi-indices you keep after truncation, $\mathcal{A}_m=\{\alpha:\lvert\alpha\rvert\le m\}$. Each multi-index $\alpha=(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)$ is just a pair of Hermite degrees, one per coordinate (Section 18) — $\alpha_1$ says which $\psi$ to use on the first whitened coordinate, $\alpha_2$ on the second. So $\mathcal{A}_2$ is every degree-pair whose entries sum to at most 2. Enumerating by total degree:

   | total degree $\lvert\alpha\rvert$ | multi-indices | meaning |
   |---|---|---|
   | 0 | $(0,0)$ | constant — the plain Gaussian |
   | 1 | $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$ | linear in coordinate 1; linear in coordinate 2 |
   | 2 | $(2,0)$, $(1,1)$, $(0,2)$ | quadratic in 1; interaction; quadratic in 2 |

   That's $\mathcal{A}_2=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(2,0),(1,1),(0,2)\}$ — 6 multi-indices, matching the count $\binom{k+m}{m}=\binom{2+2}{2}=6$ from Section 19. Each one names a basis function via $\phi_\alpha(u)=\psi_{\alpha_1}(u_1)\psi_{\alpha_2}(u_2)$; for instance $\phi_{(1,1)}(u)=\psi_1(u_1)\psi_1(u_2)=u_1u_2$. Evaluating all six at this single $u$:

   | $\alpha$ | $\phi_\alpha(u)$ |
   |---|---|
   | $(0,0)$ | $1.0000$ |
   | $(1,0)$ | $0.7071$ |
   | $(0,1)$ | $0.7071$ |
   | $(2,0)$ | $-0.3536$ |
   | $(1,1)$ | $0.5000$ |
   | $(0,2)$ | $-0.3536$ |

   That table of 6 numbers *is* the output of step 3 — this is the feature vector $\Phi_i$ that reappears in Section 31's gradient derivation. Step 4 then takes a weighted sum of exactly these 6 numbers (weighted by the coefficients $b_{c,\alpha}$) to collapse them into the single scalar $g_c(u)$.
4. $\sum_\alpha b_{c,\alpha}\phi_\alpha$ — combine into the shape polynomial $g_c$.

   *What the pieces are.* $b_{c,\alpha}$ is the learned Hermite coefficient for component $c$ and multi-index $\alpha$ — one real number per basis function, collected into the vector $b_c$ from the parameter list $\theta=\{\pi_c,\mu_c,\Sigma_c,b_c\}$ above. This step takes the list of 6 basis values from step 3 and combines them into a **single number** by weighting each one by its coefficient and adding — an ordinary dot product, $g_c(u) = b_c^\top\Phi_i$ in the notation Section 31 uses later.

   *Finishing the example.* Suppose the learned coefficients for this component are $b_c = (1,\,0,\,0,\,0,\,0.3,\,0)$ in the same order as the table above — that is, the GMM baseline ($b_{(0,0)}=1$) plus a small interaction correction ($b_{(1,1)}=0.3$, everything else off). Then

   $$g_c(u) = 1(1.0000) + 0(0.7071) + 0(0.7071) + 0(-0.3536) + 0.3(0.5000) + 0(-0.3536) = 1.15$$

   That single number, $1.15$, is what gets squared in step 5 below.
5. $(\cdot)^2 + \varepsilon$ — square for positivity, floor for safety.
6. $\div (B_c+\varepsilon)$ — normalize, exactly, via Parseval.
7. $\times\,\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_c,\Sigma_c)$ — apply the lens to the Gaussian.
8. $\sum_c \pi_c(\cdot)$ — mix the components.

## 25. The GMM sits inside it

Set $b_{c,0}=1$ and $b_{c,\alpha}=0$ for $\alpha\ne 0$. Then $g_c(u) = \phi_0(u) = 1$, so $B_c=1$ and

$$\rho_c(u) = \frac{1+\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon} = 1$$

and the model is *exactly* a standard GMM. This gives three things at once: a principled initialization (fit a GMM first), a nested-model likelihood-ratio comparison, and a guarantee that the extra flexibility can never make the fitted likelihood worse.

## 26. What each degree means

Because Hermite polynomials are graded orthogonalized moments, each degree has a clean shape interpretation:

| Degree | Controls | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Baseline Gaussian mass | The unmodified ellipsoid |
| 1 | Shift-like asymmetry | Mass slid toward one side |
| 2 | Variance / quadratic deformation | Wider or narrower than the Gaussian |
| 3 | Skewness | One tail stretched, the other compressed |
| 4 | Kurtosis / tail weight | Peakier center + heavier tails, or flatter |
| $\ge 5$ | Fine local shape | Bumps, curvature, multi-modality within a component |

### The redundancy problem at degrees 1 and 2

Degrees 1 and 2 do jobs $\mu_c$ and $\Sigma_c$ already do. A degree-1 correction shifts mass sideways — but that's what $\mu_c$ is for. A degree-2 correction rescales — but that's $\Sigma_c$.

This is **non-identifiability**: different parameter settings produce the same density. Concretely, the optimizer can shift mass right by moving $\mu_c$, or by adjusting $b_{c,(1,0,\dots)}$, and the likelihood is indifferent. The result is a flat ridge in the objective, along which the optimizer wanders without improving the fit — slow convergence, unstable coefficients, uninterpretable output.

Two fixes, per §6 of the proposal:

- **Exclude** degrees 1 and 2 from $\mathcal{A}_m$; start Hermite terms at degree 3. Cleanest, and reduces parameter count.
- **Regularize them heavily**, pinning them near zero so $\mu_c,\Sigma_c$ own those effects.

The recommended reading of the resulting fit: *"the Gaussian part tells you where the cluster is and how big; the Hermite part tells you how it deviates from being an ellipsoid."*

# Part VIII — Fitting the Model

## 27. Likelihood and the latent-variable problem

Assuming the data are independent, the **log-likelihood** is

$$\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(x_i) = \sum_{i=1}^n\log(\sum_{c=1}^C \pi_c q_c(x_i)\rho_c(u_{ic}))$$

We take logs because products of $n$ small numbers underflow, and because sums are what optimizers want.

The difficulty is structural: **a logarithm of a sum.** If we knew which component generated each point, the sum would vanish and the problem would decouple into $C$ independent, easy fits. We don't. That's the latent-variable problem, and EM is the standard response.

## 28. Responsibilities: soft cluster membership

By Bayes' rule, the posterior probability that point $i$ came from component $c$ is

$$\gamma_{ic} = \Pr(z_i = c\mid x_i) = \frac{\overbrace{\pi_c}^{\text{prior}}\overbrace{q_c(x_i)\rho_c(u_{ic})}^{\text{likelihood}}}{\sum_{d=1}^C \pi_d q_d(x_i)\rho_d(u_{id})}$$

This is the **responsibility** of component $c$ for point $i$. Numerator: how plausible this component makes this point, weighted by how common the component is. Denominator: the same summed over all components, which normalizes so $\sum_c \gamma_{ic} = 1$.

Responsibilities are *soft* assignments — a point ambiguous between two clusters gets weight in both, rather than being forced into one. This softness is exactly what makes EM's updates smooth and its convergence provable. Hard assignments come only at the end, if you want them:

$$\hat z_i = \arg\max_c \gamma_{ic}$$

**Note what's new here versus a plain GMM:** the correction $\rho_c$ appears in both numerator and denominator. A point in the skewed tail of cluster 1 is now correctly claimed by cluster 1, where a plain GMM — for which that region is implausibly far out — might have handed it to a neighbor. Better shapes give better assignments, which give better shapes. That's the virtuous loop.

## 29. Why EM works (the intuition)

Optimizing $\ell(\theta)$ directly is hard. EM sidesteps it with a two-step loop:

- **E-step:** with parameters fixed, compute the responsibilities $\gamma_{ic}$.
- **M-step:** with responsibilities fixed, update parameters as if each point $i$ belonged to component $c$ with weight $\gamma_{ic}$.

The reason this is guaranteed to work: the E-step constructs a function that *touches* $\ell$ at the current parameters and lies *below* it everywhere else — a lower bound. The M-step maximizes that bound. Since you've moved to the max of something that touches $\ell$ from below, you cannot have decreased $\ell$. **Monotone improvement every iteration**, guaranteed. (This is a *minorize-maximize* scheme; it converges to a local optimum, not necessarily the global one — hence the importance of initialization.)

For the Hermite-GMM the $b_c$-update has no closed form, so the M-step is inexact — it's really **block-coordinate ascent** (generalized EM). Monotonicity survives as long as each block update doesn't decrease the objective.

## 30. The updates, block by block

**Mixture weights.** Let $N_c = \sum_i\gamma_{ic}$ — the effective number of points owned by component $c$. Then

$$\pi_c = \frac{N_c}{n}$$

Just the share of total responsibility. Exactly as in a plain GMM.

**Gaussian parameters.** Responsibility-weighted mean and covariance:

$$\mu_c = \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_i \gamma_{ic}x_i, \qquad \Sigma_c = \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_i\gamma_{ic}(x_i-\mu_c)(x_i-\mu_c)^\top + \eta I_k$$

These are the standard GMM formulas. They are **not exactly optimal here**, because $u_{ic}$ depends on $\mu_c,\Sigma_c$, so the correction term also depends on them and the true gradient has extra pieces. The proposal's position is pragmatic: the weighted update is stable, cheap, closed-form, and usually good enough as a block move. Exact joint optimization of $(\mu_c,\Sigma_c,b_c)$ is possible but much more expensive and more prone to instability.

**Hermite coefficients.** This is the genuinely new part. Maximize

$$Q_c(b_c) = \sum_i \gamma_{ic}\log\rho_c(u_{ic}) - \lambda\sum_\alpha w_\alpha b_{c,\alpha}^2$$

Expanding $\log\rho_c = \log(g_c^2+\varepsilon) - \log(B_c+\varepsilon)$:

$$Q_c(b_c) = \sum_i\gamma_{ic}\log(g_c(u_{ic})^2+\varepsilon) - N_c\log(B_c+\varepsilon) - \lambda\, b_c^\top W b_c$$

Three interpretable terms: **fit the data**, **stay normalized**, **stay smooth**.

## 31. The gradient, derived

Collect the basis functions into a feature vector $\Phi_i = (\phi_\alpha(u_{ic}))_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_m}$, so that $g_c(u_{ic}) = b_c^\top\Phi_i$ — a plain dot product. Differentiating each of the three terms:

$$\nabla_b\log((b^\top\Phi_i)^2+\varepsilon) = \frac{2(b^\top\Phi_i)}{(b^\top\Phi_i)^2+\varepsilon}\Phi_i, \qquad \nabla_b\log(\|b\|^2+\varepsilon) = \frac{2b}{\|b\|^2+\varepsilon}, \qquad \nabla_b(-\lambda b^\top Wb) = -2\lambda Wb$$

Assembling:

$$\nabla_{b_c}Q_c = \underbrace{\sum_i \gamma_{ic}\frac{2(b_c^\top\Phi_i)}{(b_c^\top\Phi_i)^2+\varepsilon}\Phi_i}_{\text{data pull}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{2N_c\,b_c}{\|b_c\|^2+\varepsilon}}_{\text{normalization pushback}} \;-\; \underbrace{2\lambda W b_c}_{\text{smoothness}}$$

The first term pushes $|g_c|$ up wherever responsibility-weighted data actually sits. The second is the normalizer refusing to let $\|b_c\|$ grow for free. The third damps high-degree wiggle. Feed this to L-BFGS, projected gradient, or normalized gradient ascent.

## 32. Insight: the first step is exact moment matching

Initialize at the GMM: $b_c = e_0 = (1,0,\dots,0)$. Then $g_c(u_{ic}) = 1$ for every $i$, and $\|b_c\|^2=1$, so

$$[\nabla_{b_c}Q_c]_\alpha = \frac{2}{1+\varepsilon}\sum_i\gamma_{ic}\phi_\alpha(u_{ic}) - \frac{2N_c}{1+\varepsilon}\delta_{\alpha 0} - 2\lambda w_\alpha \delta_{\alpha 0}$$

- For $\alpha = 0$: since $\phi_0 \equiv 1$, the sum equals $N_c$ and cancels the second term exactly. **Zero gradient.**
- For $\alpha\ne 0$: $\;[\nabla Q_c]_\alpha \propto \sum_i\gamma_{ic}\phi_\alpha(u_{ic}) = N_c\cdot\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{\gamma_c}[\phi_\alpha(u)]$

**Each coefficient moves in the direction of the responsibility-weighted empirical Hermite moment of the whitened residuals.** If the cluster is genuinely Gaussian those moments are $\approx 0$ and nothing moves — the plain GMM is a stationary point. If the cluster is skewed, $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[\psi_3]$ is large and $b_3$ moves first, automatically.

This is the model doing exactly what you'd want: **spending parameters only where the data actually deviates from Gaussian, and telling you which kind of deviation it found.**

It also yields a free diagnostic. Before committing to the full model, fit a plain GMM and compute the weighted empirical Hermite moments per component. Their magnitudes tell you which degrees are worth including — and whether the extra machinery is worth anything at all on your data.

## 33. Insight: a hidden scale degeneracy you must fix

Set $\varepsilon = 0$ and substitute $b \to cb$ for any scalar $c \ne 0$:

$$\rho(u) = \frac{(cb^\top\Phi)^2}{\|cb\|^2} = \frac{c^2(b^\top\Phi)^2}{c^2\|b\|^2} = \frac{(b^\top\Phi)^2}{\|b\|^2}$$

**Unchanged.** An entire ray of coefficient vectors gives an identical model — a one-dimensional **gauge freedom**. Equivalently, both likelihood terms are homogeneous of degree $-1$ in $b$, so $b^\top\nabla_b(\text{log-lik part}) = 0$ identically: the likelihood gradient is always orthogonal to $b$.

Consequences, all bad if ignored:

- The Hessian is singular along $b$; Newton-type methods misbehave.
- With $\varepsilon>0$ the degeneracy is only *weakly* broken, so the problem is **ill-conditioned** rather than cleanly degenerate — arguably worse for L-BFGS, which sees a near-flat direction and thrashes.
- The ridge penalty does break it, but by pulling $\|b\|\to 0$ — a scale artifact, not meaningful shrinkage. Your $\lambda$ ends up doing two unrelated jobs at once, which makes it impossible to tune sensibly.

**Fix: pin the gauge.** Constrain $\|b_c\| = 1$ and optimize on the unit sphere. Then $\rho_c = g_c^2 + \varepsilon$ with no denominator, the regularizer genuinely penalizes high-degree content *relative to* low-degree (which is what you meant), and the projected gradient $\nabla Q - (b^\top\nabla Q)b$ is essentially free given the orthogonality noted above. This is what "normalized gradient ascent" in §8.4 is implicitly reaching for; making it explicit is worth doing.

## 34. The algorithm end to end

```
Input: data x₁..xₙ, components C, degree m, regularization λ, floor ε

1. Fit a standard GMM  →  initial πc, μc, Σc
2. Set bc = (1,0,...,0)          # ρc ≡ 1, exactly a GMM
3. repeat:
     a. for each i, c:  uic = Σc^{-1/2}(xi - μc)
                        Φi  = (φα(uic))α          # cache these
                        compute qc(xi), ρc(uic)
     b. γic ← πc qc(xi) ρc(uic) / Σd (...)        # E-step
     c. Nc ← Σi γic ;  πc ← Nc/n
     d. μc, Σc ← weighted Gaussian updates (+ ηI)
     e. bc ← argmax Qc(bc)     # L-BFGS on the sphere, warm-started
   until log-likelihood improvement < tolerance
4. Optional hard assignment:  ẑi = argmaxc γic
```

**Cost per iteration.** $O(nCk^2)$ for whitening and Gaussian densities, plus $O(nC|\mathcal{A}_m|)$ for the basis features, plus the inner optimization. Crucially, the $\Phi_i$ depend only on $u_{ic}$ and can be **cached across inner iterations** of step (e); each inner gradient evaluation is then just $O(n|\mathcal{A}_m|)$ dot products. In moderate $k$ the $\binom{k+m}{m}$ factor dominates everything else.

**Practical notes.** Work in log-space with the log-sum-exp trick for responsibilities. Warm-start each $b_c$ optimization from the previous iterate. Run only a few inner steps per outer iteration — full convergence of the inner problem is wasted effort when the responsibilities are about to change anyway.

# Part IX — Regularization and Model Selection

## 35. Why regularization is mandatory here

Two forces make it necessary. First, the parameter count $\binom{k+m}{m}$ per component, growing fast. Second — and less obvious — **high-degree polynomials oscillate**. A degree-8 polynomial can wiggle enough to put a sharp spike on each individual data point. The likelihood adores this; it is pure overfitting.

The countermeasure is degree-dependent weights that penalize higher orders more:

$$w_\alpha = |\alpha|^2 \quad\text{or}\quad w_\alpha = |\alpha|^4$$

Under $w_\alpha=|\alpha|^2$, a degree-4 term is penalized 16× more than degree-1; under $|\alpha|^4$, 256×. This expresses a genuine prior belief: **clusters are probably close to Gaussian, and high-frequency structure is more likely noise than signal.** It's the same logic as a smoothness penalty in splines or weight decay favoring simple functions.

Note these depend only on total degree $|\alpha|$, which (Section 14) makes them invariant to the arbitrary choice of matrix square root. That's not an accident — it's the right design.

## 36. Choosing $C$, $m$, $\lambda$

**Held-out log-likelihood.** Fit on training data, evaluate

$$\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}|}\sum_{x_i\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{val}}}\log p_\theta(x_i)$$

The most direct measure: does the model predict data it has never seen? Overfitting shows up unambiguously as a falling validation score while training likelihood climbs.

**BIC.** When you can't spare data for validation:

$$\text{BIC} = -2\ell(\hat\theta) + d_{\text{eff}}\log n$$

Lower is better. It trades fit against complexity, with the penalty growing as $\log n$. The effective parameter count:

$$d_{\text{eff}} = \underbrace{(C-1)}_{\text{weights}} + \underbrace{Ck}_{\text{means}} + \underbrace{C\tfrac{k(k+1)}{2}}_{\text{covariances}} + \underbrace{C|\mathcal{A}_m|}_{\text{Hermite}}$$

($C-1$ because the weights sum to 1; $k(k+1)/2$ because covariance matrices are symmetric.) Subtract any excluded low-degree coefficients. Treat this as approximate — with regularization the *effective* degrees of freedom are fewer than the raw count, so BIC over-penalizes somewhat.

**Cluster stability.** Refit on bootstrap resamples and check how consistent the assignments are. Genuine structure is stable; overfitted structure is not. This is an especially good check here, because the whole promise of the model is *better clusters*, not just better likelihood — and those are separable outcomes worth verifying separately.

**A recommended search order**, exploiting the nesting: fit plain GMMs first and pick $C$ by BIC or validation. Then, holding $C$, raise $m$ from 0 upward and stop when validation likelihood stops improving. Then tune $\lambda$. This is far cheaper than a full grid and rarely much worse.

# Part X — Changing the Reference: The General Recipe

## 37. Nothing was Gaussian-specific

Return to Part III. The density-ratio argument used only two properties of the Gaussian: that the target is absolutely continuous with respect to it, and that we had an orthonormal basis for its $L^2$ space. Neither is unique to Gaussians. So the construction generalizes wholesale:

1. Pick a reference measure $Q_c$ whose support covers the target ($P_c \ll Q_c$).
2. Find an orthonormal system $\{\varphi_j\}$ in $L^2(Q_c)$.
3. Set $g_c = \sum_{j=1}^M b_{cj}\varphi_j$ and $\rho_c = \dfrac{g_c^2+\varepsilon}{\sum_j b_{cj}^2+\varepsilon}$.
4. Then $\int\rho_c\,dQ_c = 1$ by the identical Parseval argument, and $\rho_c Q_c$ is a valid probability law.

**Gaussian + Hermite is one instance of a template, not the template itself.**

## 38. The menu

| Reference | Natural basis | Right for |
|---|---|---|
| Gaussian on $\mathbb{R}^k$ | Hermite products | Euclidean data, roughly Gaussian clusters |
| Student-$t$ | Orthogonal polys for the $t$ weight | Heavy-tailed clusters |
| Gamma on $\mathbb{R}_+$ | Laguerre | Positive quantities: durations, waiting times |
| Beta on $[0,1]$ | Jacobi | Bounded proportions, rates |
| Uniform on interval | Legendre | Bounded Euclidean domains |
| Circle / torus | Fourier | Angles, time-of-day, periodic variables |
| Sphere | Spherical harmonics | Directional data, unit vectors |

Each pairing is the same relationship as Gaussian↔Hermite: the basis is the one orthogonal under that particular weight.

## 39. Three things the reference choice determines

**Support** — a hard constraint, not a preference. If $Q_c(A)=0$ but $P_c(A)>0$ then no density ratio exists on $A$ (Section 8), and no amount of fitting will fix it. A Beta reference cannot produce mass outside $[0,1]$. The Gaussian's full support is precisely why it's a safe default.

**Tail behavior** — the reference sets the baseline decay, and the correction can only modulate it polynomially. A Gaussian dies like $e^{-\|u\|^2/2}$; multiplying by a degree-$m$ polynomial-squared still dies essentially that fast. **So you cannot make a Gaussian reference into a genuinely heavy-tailed distribution.** Trying forces huge high-degree coefficients that oscillate wildly in the bulk, wrecking the fit where most of your data lives. For heavy tails, change the reference to Student-$t$ — its baseline already assigns real probability far out, so the correction only has to handle shape.

**Domain geometry** — if data live on a curved or bounded space, a Euclidean reference is fighting the geometry. Directional data on a sphere want a spherical reference with spherical harmonics; angles want Fourier. Forcing them into $\mathbb{R}^k$ creates artificial discontinuities (e.g. 359° and 1° looking maximally distant).

## 40. The guiding principle

$$\text{choose } Q_c \text{ so that } R_c = \frac{dP_c}{dQ_c} \text{ is simple}$$

Worth stating carefully: the goal is **not** a reference "close to" the data in some vague sense, and **not** a complete basis. It is that the *ratio* be low-degree or sparse in your chosen basis. Completeness guarantees you can approximate anything eventually; it says nothing about doing so with 10 coefficients instead of 10,000.

This is the same instinct as choosing good features in regression or a good proposal in importance sampling. **Put the known structure in the reference; learn only what's left.**

# Part XI — A Fully Worked Example

Abstraction becomes concrete fastest in one dimension. Let $k=1$ and take a single component.

Here $x$ is what it has been throughout: **one data point** — one of the observations $x_1,\dots,x_n$ from Section 1. In $k=1$ dimension it's just a single real number rather than a vector, so this example is the case where each observation is one measurement (a reaction time, an income, a test score) rather than a list of several.

The whitened coordinate simplifies accordingly. In general it's $u_c(x)=\Sigma_c^{-1/2}(x-\mu_c)$ (Section 12), but with $k=1$ the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ is a $1\times1$ matrix — i.e. just the variance $\sigma^2$ — so $\Sigma^{-1/2}$ is the ordinary scalar $1/\sigma$, and the matrix–vector product becomes plain division:

$$u = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$

which is exactly the familiar z-score: *how many standard deviations is this point from the mean.* (This is the 1-D case of the Mahalanobis reading in Section 13.) Dropping the component subscript $c$ since there's only one component.

Following Section 26's advice to skip degrees 1 and 2, turn on only the cubic term: $b = (1,0,0,\beta)$, so

$$g(u) = 1 + \beta\,\frac{u^3-3u}{\sqrt6}, \qquad \rho(u) = \frac{g(u)^2}{1+\beta^2}$$

One free parameter, $\beta$, controlling skew.

## 41. Working out the moments by hand

Expand: $g^2 = 1 + \tfrac{2\beta}{\sqrt6}(u^3-3u) + \tfrac{\beta^2}{6}(u^3-3u)^2$.

**Normalization.** $\mathbb{E}[g^2] = 1+\beta^2$ by orthonormality (the cross term $\mathbb{E}[\psi_0\psi_3]=0$). Dividing by $1+\beta^2$ gives exactly 1 — Parseval, no integration.

**Mean.** $\mathbb{E}[u\rho]$: the linear cross-term is $\mathbb{E}[\psi_1\psi_3]=0$ by orthogonality; $u(u^3-3u)^2$ is an odd function so it integrates to zero. **The mean stays at 0** — the cubic correction doesn't fight $\mu$.

**Second moment.** $\mathbb{E}[u^2(u^3-3u)]=0$ (odd). And $\mathbb{E}[u^2(u^6-6u^4+9u^2)] = 105-90+27=42$. So

$$\mathbb{E}[u^2] = \frac{1+7\beta^2}{1+\beta^2}$$

**Third moment.** Write $u^3 = \mathrm{He}_3+3\mathrm{He}_1$, so $\mathbb{E}[u^3\mathrm{He}_3]=3!=6$; the $\beta^2$ term is odd and vanishes. So

$$\mathbb{E}[u^3] = \frac{2\sqrt6\,\beta}{1+\beta^2}\approx\frac{4.899\beta}{1+\beta^2}$$

Confirmed by numerical integration:

| $\beta$ | norm | $\mathbb{E}[u]$ | $\mathbb{E}[u^2]$ | $\mathbb{E}[u^3]$ | std. skewness |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 1.00000 | 0 | 1.000 | 0.000 | 0.000 |
| 0.1 | 1.00000 | 0 | 1.059 | 0.485 | 0.445 |
| 0.2 | 1.00000 | 0 | 1.231 | 0.942 | 0.690 |
| 0.3 | 1.00000 | 0 | 1.495 | 1.348 | **0.737** |
| 0.5 | 1.00000 | 0 | 2.200 | 1.960 | 0.601 |
| 1.0 | 1.00000 | 0 | 4.000 | 2.449 | 0.306 |

## 42. Three lessons from the numbers

**(a) It works, and it's cheap.** One extra parameter buys standardized skewness up to $\approx 0.74$, and the normalization column is exactly 1.000 throughout — by construction, never computed. That's the payoff of the whole orthonormal-plus-squaring design.

**(b) Squaring is non-monotone.** Skewness *peaks* near $\beta\approx0.3$ and then **falls back toward zero**. Because $\rho\propto g^2$, large $\beta$ lets the symmetric $\beta^2 g_3^2$ term dominate. So the map from coefficients to shape is non-injective and non-monotone, and the objective in $b$ is genuinely non-convex with multiple basins. Practical implication: **use multiple restarts, and don't interpret a large coefficient as a large effect.**

**(c) Degrees couple more than advertised.** The cubic term inflates variance from 1.00 to 1.50 at $\beta=0.3$ — a 50% change from a term nominally about *skewness*. In EM, $\Sigma_c$ and $b_c$ therefore fight: the weighted covariance update shrinks $\sigma$ to compensate, which changes $u$, which changes the fitted $b$. §6 of the proposal flags this for degrees 1–2; it applies at degree 3+ too, just more weakly. Expect slower convergence than a plain GMM.

## 43. The node problem, made concrete

$g(u)$ is a cubic, so it has real roots, and $\rho = g^2/(1+\beta^2)$ vanishes there:

| $\beta$ | 0.1 | 0.3 | 0.5 | 1.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| node at $u=$ | −3.25 | −2.50 | −2.27 | −2.05 |

So the fitted density has a **hard zero about 2.5 standard deviations into the left tail, with density rising again beyond it.** That is an artifact of squaring, not a feature of any real data — a small phantom gap followed by a phantom bump.

This is exactly what $\varepsilon$ is for. It floors the density there so a single observation landing on a node doesn't send the log-likelihood to $-\infty$. It's worth checking your fitted $g_c$ for roots inside the data range and treating their presence as a warning sign of an over-flexible fit.

# Part XII — Honest Assessment

## 44. What the model genuinely delivers

- **Exact normalization in closed form.** No partition function, ever. This is the central achievement, and it's not easy to get.
- **One component per cluster, preserved.** Interpretability survives non-Gaussian shape.
- **Strict, safe extension of the GMM.** Nested for model comparison; can't do worse; initializes trivially.
- **Interpretable, invariant diagnostics.** Degree-block energies say *how* a cluster deviates from Gaussian, and don't depend on implementation choices.
- **Familiar computational shape.** Slots into EM; each piece is a small modification of code you already have.
- **A general template.** Part X's recipe applies far beyond Gaussians.

## 45. What it costs

- **Dimension.** $\binom{k+m}{m}$ per component. Comfortable to maybe $k\approx 5$–10 with low $m$; beyond that you need structural restrictions (diagonal-only multi-indices, shared coefficients across components, low-rank structure) that the proposal doesn't specify.
- **Non-convexity, amplified.** GMMs already have multiple local optima; squaring adds more, and the parameter-to-shape map is non-monotone. Multiple restarts are not optional.
- **The scale gauge (Section 33),** which will silently degrade your optimizer unless you pin it.
- **Nodes (Section 43)** — spurious zeros in the density, needing $\varepsilon$ and monitoring.
- **Parameter coupling (Section 42c)** between $\Sigma_c$ and low/mid-degree Hermite terms, slowing convergence.
- **Light tails, structurally.** Section 39: a Gaussian reference cannot be corrected into a heavy-tailed law. Use a $t$ reference instead.
- **Approximate M-step.** The $\mu_c,\Sigma_c$ update ignores the correction's dependence on them, so monotonicity is not strictly guaranteed by the standard argument. Track the likelihood and verify it in practice.

## 46. When to reach for it

Good fit when: you have moderate dimension ($k\lesssim10$); you have a genuine reason to expect skewed or otherwise non-ellipsoidal clusters; **cluster interpretability matters** (you want one component per real group); and you want a normalized, likelihood-based model supporting principled model comparison.

Look elsewhere when: dimension is high (try mixtures of factor analyzers, or a deep generative model); tails are heavy (mixtures of $t$-distributions); the shape deviation is a known parametric form (skew-normal or skew-$t$ are cheaper and better-behaved); or you only care about assignments and not density (spectral clustering, DBSCAN).

The honest summary: **it occupies a real and useful niche — moderate dimension, interpretability required, mild non-Gaussianity — and its central technical achievement is closed-form exact normalization of a flexible positive correction, obtained by chaining a whitening transform, an orthonormal basis, and a squaring trick.** Every piece of machinery in the proposal exists to make that one thing work.

# Appendix B — The Logical Chain in One Page

1. GMM components are ellipsoids; real clusters often aren't. **[§2]**
2. Adding components destroys interpretability, so instead multiply each Gaussian by a correction. **[§4]**
3. The correction must be $\ge 0$ and average to exactly 1 under the Gaussian. **[§5]**
4. Such a correction always exists — it's the Radon–Nikodym derivative $dP_c/dQ_c$, guaranteed because the Gaussian has full support. **[§8–9]**
5. Chaining densities gives $p_c = q_c R_c$: an actual pdf you can put in a likelihood. **[§10]**
6. Whiten to $u_c = \Sigma_c^{-1/2}(x-\mu_c)$ so every component's reference is the *standard* Gaussian and one fixed basis serves all. **[§12–13]**
7. The basis orthonormal under the standard Gaussian is the product Hermite basis. **[§17–18]**
8. Truncate by total degree — the natural, rotation-invariant cut. **[§19]**
9. An additive correction can go negative; an exponential one has no closed-form normalizer. **[§20–21]**
10. **Square it.** Positivity is automatic, and Parseval turns the normalizing integral into $\sum_\alpha b_\alpha^2$ — exact, closed-form, for every parameter value. **[§22]**
11. Add $\varepsilon$ so polynomial roots don't send $\log\rho$ to $-\infty$. **[§23]**
12. $b_c=(1,0,\dots,0)$ recovers the GMM exactly: safe init, nested comparison. **[§25]**
13. Fit by EM; the $b_c$-block needs gradient ascent, and its first step is exactly empirical-Hermite-moment matching. **[§30–32]**
14. Pin the scale gauge, regularize by degree, select $(C,m,\lambda)$ by held-out likelihood. **[§33, §35–36]**
15. None of this was Gaussian-specific — swap in any reference with an orthonormal basis, chosen so the *ratio* is simple. **[§37–40]**
